# Video part 1: customer pitch (about 4 minutes 30 seconds)

Spoken text is plain. Bracketed lines are things to do on screen. Every number is from the committed snapshot.

[0:00 Open the app on the Decision tab. Delivery day Thu 15 Jan 2026, cutoff 09:20, cost ratio 3. Stay on the chart.]

It is 09:20 on Wednesday 14 January. In a few minutes you have to commit to tomorrow's day-ahead purchase, and the first question is how much wind you can count on.

This screen answers it. The forecast you can use is the latest one published before the cutoff. That is the 07:30 issue. The next one lands at 09:30, ten minutes too late, and the page says so instead of quietly using it.

[0:30 Point at the 11.6 GW figure, then the row of numbers beside it.]

For Thursday the forecast averages 11.6 gigawatts. It is lowest between 4 pm and 7 pm, at 8.9 gigawatts. That is the stretch where you lean hardest on thermal plants.

[1:00 Point at the shaded range, then the bars of extra cover underneath.]

A forecast is one line, but you buy against a risk. The shaded range is built only from how wrong past forecasts were, using days that were complete before this morning. Nothing from the future is in it.

[1:25 Change the cost ratio from 3 to 1, then back to 3.]

Here is where your judgement comes in. If being short costs you three times what a surplus does, the app suggests covering enough to be safe in three out of four half-hours. That is 41,118 megawatt-hours of extra cover across the day, mostly between noon and 3 pm, about 1.8 gigawatts. Change the ratio and the cover moves. No prices are assumed. The ratio is yours.

[1:55 Tick Hindsight view.]

Now the hindsight view, which you cannot see on the day. Actual wind averaged 10.6 gigawatts against a forecast of 11.6. The forecast was about a gigawatt high, and all 48 half-hours landed inside the range.

[2:20 Click the Accuracy tab. Rest on the first row of numbers, then the chart.]

One good day proves little, so here is all of January. 1,488 half-hours. The forecast missed by 2,042 megawatts on average, which is 18 percent of the wind actually generated. The more useful fact is the direction. The forecast was too high in 85 percent of half-hours, by 1.7 gigawatts on average. For a buyer that is the expensive side.

[2:55 Scroll to the error by hour chart, then the table of error by forecast level.]

The misses are not even across the day. They are largest around 5 am, at 2,436 megawatts, and smallest at 9 pm, at 1,788. They are also worst when the forecast is high. Above about 16.7 gigawatts the average miss is 2,930 megawatts, nearly double the low-wind case. When the forecast promises a lot of wind, that is when to be most careful.

[3:10 Go back to Decision. Pick Wed 28 Jan 2026. Hindsight stays on.]

And there are bad days. On 28 January the forecast said 11.1 gigawatts and 5.8 arrived. No half-hour that day landed inside the range. I would rather show you this than hide it.

[3:35 Click the Cutoff sensitivity tab.]

You might ask whether the exact cutoff time matters. We tested four. Forecasts are issued at 07:30 and 09:30, so the four cutoffs collapse into two answers. The 09:50 and 11:00 cutoffs use the newer forecast and are only 26 megawatts better. In January, the clock is not what drives your risk. The age of the forecast matters more. A forecast made at least 48 hours ahead misses by 2,316 megawatts on average, and one made at the last moment misses by 1,687. The chart shows that curve, so you can see what a few hours of waiting is worth.

[3:55 Click Data and method. Click Download CSV.]

Finally, every number comes with a receipt. This file lists each half-hour, which forecast issue it used, and a hash of the inputs. Your analysts can re-run it and get the same answer, which is what makes the numbers defensible in a review.

[4:15 Return to the Accuracy headline.]

What the pilot showed: out of sample, the range held in 79.8 percent of half-hours, against the 80 percent it promises. One limit, stated plainly. We compare forecasts with metered output after curtailment, and this data cannot separate that from forecast error. That is the first thing I would test next.
